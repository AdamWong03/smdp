     Thermodynamically consistent force-free molecular dynamics via a
              mode-resolved generalized Langevin readout
                                  [Author list and affiliations to be supplied]


 1                                                     Abstract
 2             Force-free direct propagators can accelerate molecular dynamics by large factors, but their
 3         long-time equilibrium statistics are unreliable. We show that pointwise one-step accuracy is
 4         neither necessary nor sufficient for long-time fidelity: a published propagator with a large point-
 5         wise error reproduces the canonical water ensemble, while a pointwise-accurate model fails. The
 6         failure is a mode-resolved stationary-measure problem, not an NVE energy problem. We in-
 7         troduce a mode-resolved generalized Langevin readout (GLR): a frozen propagator supplies the
 8         displacement, while per-mode AR(p) memory kernels fitted from the reference velocity autocor-
 9         relation, fluctuation–dissipation-consistent noise, and exact per-mode variance rescaling enforce
10         the canonical mean kinetic partition by construction. On water, the GLR matches multi-lag
11         velocity correlations, reproduces the O–O/O–H/H–H radial distribution functions with RMSEs
12         of 0.031/0.038/0.028 and the O vibrational-density-of-states overlap of 0.953, better than the
13         published checkpoint (0.043/0.118/0.096 and 0.937), is stable and initial-condition independent,
14         and yields a diffusion coefficient of 1.39–1.41 ×10−9 m2 s−1 on two starts, within 26% of the
15         reference 1.84–1.88 ×10−9 m2 s−1 and far closer than the AR(1) readout (3.59 ×10−9 ). The
16         readout is backbone-agnostic, training-free, and force-free.


17   1    Introduction
18   Molecular dynamics (MD) is the workhorse for connecting microscopic interactions to the macro-
19   scopic behaviour of liquids, solids, and biological assemblies. Its central limitation is time: the
20   1–2 fs integration step required for stable dynamics restricts routine simulations to nanoseconds,
21   while many phenomena of interest—diffusion, nucleation, conformational transitions, and slow
22   relaxation—unfold over microseconds to milliseconds. Machine-learned interatomic potentials have
23   reduced the cost of the force evaluation, but they retain the sequential integrator and therefore the
24   same step-count bottleneck.
25       A more radical route is to learn the propagator itself. Instead of evaluating forces and integrating
26   Newton’s equations, a direct propagator maps a state at time t to a state at t + ∆t with ∆t several
27   times the stable integration step. Because the map is force-free, it can in principle be evaluated at
28   a fraction of the cost of an MD step and can be rolled out autoregressively. TrajCast demonstrated
29   that this idea is viable for liquid water: an equivariant message-passing network trained on 5 fs
30   pairs reproduces the oxygen–oxygen radial distribution function (RDF), the vibrational density of
31   states (VDOS), and the diffusion coefficient under a global thermostat 1 . The result is an important
32   existence proof: faithful long-time water physics is possible without forces.
33       What the existence proof does not provide is a guarantee. A direct propagator is trained on
34   one-step pairs, usually by minimizing a displacement or velocity error. The quantity that matters at
35   long times, however, is the stationary measure of the composed map—the distribution of positions
36   and velocities that the autoregressive rollout visits after transients have decayed. One-step accuracy
37   constrains a conditional mean; it says little about the invariant measure of a nonlinear, stochastic,

                                                           1
38   or thermostatted iteration. The gap is familiar in spirit from generative modelling, where a low
39   reconstruction error does not imply a correct sample distribution, but it has not been diagnosed
40   systematically for force-free MD propagators.
41       Here we show that the gap is real and that it has a specific structure. Using water as a test sys-
42   tem, we compare a published TrajCast checkpoint with our own direct propagator. The published
43   model has a one-step position error about fifteen times larger than ours on held-out transitions, yet
44   it reproduces the canonical mode partition, RDF, and VDOS, whereas our pointwise-better model
45   develops a large mode imbalance and fails the same 100 ps NVT screen. The ranking by one-step
46   error is therefore not just noisy—it can be inverted. A second diagnostic removes another plausible
47   explanation: 25 ps NVE energy drift does not order the models either. The published model heats
48   strongly in NVE and still passes NVT; our most NVE-stable model fails NVT. The relevant object
49   is the composed propagator plus thermostat, and the relevant failure is a bias in the mode-resolved
50   stationary measure, not a one-step error or an energy-integration error.
51       Existing remedies attack different parts of this problem. Global stochastic thermostats, such
52   as CSVR, control the total kinetic energy but not its distribution among molecular translation,
53   rotation, and internal motion 2 . Structure-preserving learned maps enforce symplectic or time-
54   reversible structure and improve energy conservation and equipartition 3 , but they are deterministic
55   and target mechanical structure rather than the canonical NVT measure. Learned velocity updates
56   recover stability by injecting an LSTM prediction periodically 4 , but the update is a black box and
57   is not tied to the equilibrium mode partition. Generalized Langevin equation (GLE) thermostats
58   provide memory kernels and colored noise with the correct fluctuation–dissipation relation 5,6 , and
59   frequency-resolved thermostats can control selected modes 7 , but these are thermostats for an MD
60   integrator, not readouts for a frozen force-free propagator. Stochastic and generative propagators
61   offer another route—Timewarp accelerates dynamics with learned time-coarsening 8 , and generative
62   models such as MDGen sample trajectory distributions 9 —at the cost of MCMC, an energy function,
63   or a substantially larger generative model.
64       We take a different decomposition. A frozen force-free backbone is responsible for structure: it
65   supplies the displacement ∆qt . A lightweight, mode-resolved generalized-Langevin readout (GLR)
66   is responsible for thermodynamics: it supplies the velocity that enters the next backbone call and
67   the kinetic diagnostics. The readout decomposes the velocity into molecular translation, rotation,
68   and internal modes; fits a per-mode AR(p) memory kernel to the reference velocity autocorrelation
69   by Yule–Walker; injects fluctuation–dissipation-consistent noise with the prediction-error variance;
70   and rescales each mode to its canonical mean kinetic energy Na kB T /2 at every step. The partition
71   is therefore enforced by construction, while the memory kernel matches the multi-lag dynamics
72   that a single gain cannot capture. The backbone is not retrained, and the readout is fitted from a
73   short reference NVT trajectory.
74       We apply the GLR to SPC/Fw water 10 and compare it with the published TrajCast checkpoint,
75   our 250k direct propagator, an AR(1) readout, and several negative controls. The GLR is stable
76   for 100 ps from two independent initial conditions, is initial-condition independent in its partition,
77   ACF, RDF, and VDOS, and reproduces the O–O, O–H, and H–H RDFs and the O VDOS more
78   closely than the published checkpoint in our analysis. The 2.5 ns production run tests the diffusion
79   coefficient—the last long-time observable—and the ablations isolate the roles of the memory order,
80   the FD noise, the variance rescale, and the displacement coupling. The result is a general recipe for
81   making a force-free propagator thermodynamically consistent without retraining it, and a diagnostic
82   that explains when and why such a propagator will fail.

 94   2     Results
 95   2.1    Pointwise accuracy does not order long-time fidelity
 96   We first asked whether the conventional training objective—one-step displacement or velocity error
 97   on held-out transitions—predicts the long-time behaviour of a force-free propagator. We compared
 98   two models trained on the same SPC/Fw water data: our 250k relational propagator and the
 99   published TrajCast water checkpoint. On 1,250 held-out transitions, the published checkpoint has
100   a mean absolute position error of 4.75 × 10−3 Å and a velocity error of 1.63 × 10−3 Å fs−1 . The
101   250k model is about fifteen times more accurate pointwise (2.97 × 10−4 Å and 1.09 × 10−4 Å fs−1 ).
102   If one-step accuracy controlled long-time fidelity, the 250k model should be the better sampler.
103       It is not. Under the same 100 ps NVT protocol (300 K, CSVR, 5 fs), the published checkpoint
104   reproduces the canonical kinetic partition—translation 2.505 eV, rotation 2.463 eV, internal 2.444
105   eV against the 2.5 ns reference means of 2.459, 2.502, and 2.450 eV (the first 100 ps reference block
106   is an outlier at 2.373/2.430/2.609 eV)—and gives O–O, O–H, and H–H RDF RMSEs of 0.043,
107   0.118, and 0.096. The 250k model, despite its lower one-step error, collapses the collective modes
108   and overheats the internal mode: translation 0.538 eV, rotation 0.786 eV, internal 6.044 eV. Its
109   RDF RMSEs are 0.646, 1.312, and 0.249. The total kinetic energy is close to the target in both
110   cases; the distribution across modes is not. A pointwise-better model is a worse sampler, and the
111   failure is invisible to the training loss.
112       The inversion is not a peculiarity of one checkpoint. A 20k-step prefix of the same architecture
113   is even more pointwise accurate but becomes unstable in NVT after about 50 ps. A native TrajCast
114   architecture trained to only about 10% of its schedule has a pointwise error three times smaller than
115   the 250k model and fails in the opposite direction—translation 3.256 eV, rotation 3.105 eV, internal
116   1.041 eV. The sign of the mode bias is architecture- and training-dependent, but its presence is
117   not.

118   2.2    NVE energy drift is a red herring
119   A natural second hypothesis is that the failing models inject energy and heat, and that a better
120   energy integrator would fix the ensemble. We tested this directly with 25 ps NVE rollouts from
121   the same initial state. The published checkpoint heats from 302 K to 476 K; the 250k model
122   cools from 302 K to 271 K; the NVT-pair fine-tuned M0 model heats to 387 K. The ordering by
123   NVE drift is essentially the reverse of the ordering by NVT fidelity: the model that heats the

                                                        3
124   most passes the NVT screen, and the most NVE-stable model fails it. Energy conservation and
125   canonical sampling are different properties of the composed map. A force-free propagator can be
126   a poor energy integrator and a good thermostat, or vice versa; NVE drift cannot be used as a
127   surrogate for the stationary measure.

128   2.3   The failure is a mode-resolved partition bias
129   The two diagnostics point to a common variable: the distribution of kinetic energy among molecular
130   modes. Figure 1c shows the mode kinetic energy relative to the reference for all models. The failing
131   models are not simply hot or cold. They are partitioned: the 250k family is collectively cold and
132   internally hot (translation 0.23, rotation 0.32, internal 2.32 of the reference), the native/M0 family
133   is collectively hot and internally cold (1.37, 1.28, 0.40), and the published checkpoint is close to the
134   reference on all three (1.06, 1.01, 0.94). The total temperature is approximately 300 K in every case.
135   A global thermostat holds the scalar temperature while leaving the vector of mode temperatures
136   biased.
137       This resolves the apparent paradox. The one-step loss is dominated by the conditional mean
138   of the displacement, which is insensitive to a slow redistribution of kinetic energy across modes.
139   The NVE trajectory measures the total energy budget, which can drift without changing the mode
140   partition in the same way. The NVT stationary measure is the object that sees the bias, and the
141   mode partition is its most informative low-dimensional projection.

142   2.4   Stability is not correctness
143   The distinction matters because long rollouts are often used as the acceptance test. Figure 1d plots
144   the longest stable NVT run against the partition verdict. The phase-space v0 model is stable for
145   2.75 ns and has a partition of 0.111/0.808/6.338 eV; the 250k and Current-Only models are stable
146   for 150 ps with the same bias. Stability without partition correctness is a long-lived wrong answer,
147   not a success. Conversely, short-horizon fixes can destabilize: the M0 endpoint fine-tune passes on
148   the val 04 start but blows up at 130 ps on an independent start, and the 5-step CSRV moment loss
149   blows up at 4.3 ps. The acceptance criterion must be the stationary measure, not the absence of a
150   numerical crash.
151       These results define the design requirement for a remedy. The propagator must be paired with
152   a readout that controls the mode-resolved kinetic partition and the multi-lag dynamics, not just
153   the total temperature or a single-lag gain. The readout should not require retraining the backbone,
154   and it should be evaluated on the same long-time observables that expose the failure. The next
155   section introduces such a readout.

191   2.6   Water NVT
192   We applied the GLR to the frozen 250k backbone that failed the NVT screen without it. The
193   readout was fitted once from a 100 ps block of the reference NVT trajectory (500–600 ps) and
194   then used unchanged in all rollouts. We ran 100 ps from the val 04 NVE initial condition and
195   from an independent NVT trajectory (301.8 K), and a 2.5 ns production run from each start.
196   The integration step is 5 fs; no external thermostat is applied—the GLR is the thermostat. We
197   compare the mode kinetic partition, the mode velocity ACFs, the O–O/O–H/H–H RDFs, the H/O
198   VDOS, and the molecular centre-of-mass MSD and diffusion coefficient against the reference NVT
199   trajectory.
200       Without the readout, the 250k backbone has translation 0.538 eV, rotation 0.786 eV, and
201   internal 6.044 eV. With the GLR, the 100 ps partition is 2.482/2.526/2.399 eV from val 04 and
202   2.445/2.490/2.362 eV from the independent start, against the 2.5 ns reference means of 2.459/2.502/2.450
203   eV. The total temperature is 300.0 K in both runs. The two starts agree within the reference block-
204   to-block scatter, so the stationary partition is not a property of the val 04 initial condition. The
205   published checkpoint gives 2.505/2.463/2.444 eV, also correct but with a different mix of residuals.
206       The per-mode ACFs tell the same story. The translation ACF matches the reference at 5,
207   25, 100, and 500 fs (GLR 0.981/0.640/0.014/−0.030 versus reference 0.982/0.658/0.039/−0.019).
208   The internal ACF matches at 5 fs (−0.575 versus −0.567) and reproduces the mid-lag oscillation;
209   the 500 fs tail is still too persistent (0.312 versus 0.104), a known limitation of AR(10). The
210   AR(20) fit removes most of that residual (internal 100/200 fs 0.147/0.068 versus 0.147/0.072) and
211   is reported as an order ablation rather than the main configuration. The readout matches the
212   multi-lag dynamics, not just a single 5 fs gain.

243   position update remains the limiting factor. We therefore report the 25% deviation as a limitation
244   rather than claiming exact diffusion recovery.
245       The GLR is the first configuration in this study that combines (i) a stable 2.5 ns NVT run, (ii)
246   a canonical mean mode partition, (iii) multi-lag ACF agreement, and (iv) RDF and VDOS at or
247   beyond the published checkpoint. Its diffusion coefficient is 25% low but qualitatively correct, and
248   far closer than the AR(1) readout. It does so with a frozen backbone and a readout fitted from 100
249   ps of reference data. The remaining questions—seed error bars, the internal long tail, the diffusion
250   deficit, and a second system—are addressed in the ablations and discussion.

251   2.7                   Ablations
252   The AR(1) readout is the natural minimal thermostat: one gain per mode, fitted to the 5 fs
253   correlation. It holds the mean partition once the variance is rescaled, but it does not reproduce
254   the multi-lag dynamics. At 100 ps, the O–H RDF RMSE is 0.312 for the GLR AR(1), 0.301 for
255   FDR v1, 0.131 for AR(5), 0.104 for AR(10), and 0.117 for AR(20); the O–H RDF is the clearest
256   signature. The AR(1) internal ACF decays monotonically from −0.567 to zero, while the reference
257   oscillates and crosses zero near 25 fs; the AR(10) kernel reproduces the oscillation. The AR(20)
258   kernel also captures the 100–200 fs tail (internal 100/200 fs 0.147/0.068 versus 0.147/0.072), at
259   the cost of a longer history and a larger coefficient vector, but its better long-lag ACF does not
260   translate into a better short-window O–H. AR(10) therefore remains the main configuration; the
261   order sweep shows the qualitative improvement from AR(1) to AR(10) rather than a monotone
262   gain with order.
263       The ablation that removes the per-step rescale isolates the partition guarantee. In the AR(1)
264   readout, removing the rescale collapses the total kinetic energy and cools the system to about


                                                                                                                  9
265   14 K. With the GLR readout, the no-rescale run is stable but cools to 172 K; the partition is
266   0.763/0.649/2.839 eV, the RDFs degrade to 0.398/0.474/0.279, and the H/O VDOS overlaps fall
267   to 0.432/0.608. The rescale is not a cosmetic normalization; it is the step that makes the mode
268   kinetic energy a design constraint rather than a by-product of the backbone and the noise.
269       Contrary to our expectation, removing the FD noise did not degrade the 100 ps structure: the
270   partition is unchanged (2.482/2.526/2.399 eV), the RDFs are 0.034/0.098/0.026, the H/O VDOS
271   overlaps are 0.849/0.962, and the 10–80 ps diffusion estimate is 1.55×10−9 (versus 1.45×10−9
272   with noise). At the small blend λ = 0.1, the backbone and the variance rescale provide enough
273   fluctuation that the noise term is not the empirical driver. The memory kernel and the rescale are
274   the load-bearing components; the FD noise is part of the theoretical construction and may matter
275   at larger blend or for the one-point velocity distribution, but we do not claim it is necessary on the
276   basis of this ablation.
277       For the AR(1) readout, increasing the internal blend from 0.1 to 1.0 makes the O–H RDF
278   worse (0.957 versus 0.301) and the VDOS worse (H/O 0.618/0.613 versus 0.807/0.851). A larger
279   admixture of a single-gain readout does not fix the spectrum; it replaces the backbone’s internal
280   bias with the readout’s wrong long-lag dynamics. The blend is kept at 0.1 to preserve the position
281   head; the memory kernel, not the blend, does the work.
282       Adding the velocity correction to the displacement—the obvious way to make positions and
283   velocities consistent—crashes at 24.8 ps (minimum distance 0.647 Å) while the temperature remains
284   at 300 K. The failure is structural, not thermal: the correction injects a displacement increment that
285   the backbone’s next state cannot absorb. The decoupled scheme is therefore a stability requirement,
286   and the method does not simply integrate the readout velocity.
287       Three 100 ps seeds give the same partition (2.482/2.526/2.399 eV) and a narrow spread in the
288   structural metrics: O–H 0.104/0.111/0.110, O–O 0.032/0.034/0.033, H VDOS 0.848/0.854/0.846,
289   and O VDOS 0.964/0.966/0.959. The 2.5 ns O–H improvement to 0.038 is therefore a sampling
290   effect of the longer trajectory, not a seed fluctuation. The frozen-vs-end-to-end backbone compar-
291   ison and a second system are left to future work: the present paper is a water method paper, and
292   the claim is architectural rather than universal.


293   3    Discussion
294   We have shown that the long-time failure of force-free molecular dynamics propagators is not
295   primarily a one-step accuracy problem. A published propagator with a fifteen-fold larger pointwise
296   error reproduces the canonical water ensemble, while a pointwise-better model develops a large
297   mode imbalance and fails the same NVT screen. The failure is also not an energy-drift problem:
298   the model that heats most strongly in NVE passes NVT, and the most NVE-stable model fails.
299   The discriminating object is the mode-resolved stationary measure of the composed propagator
300   plus thermostat. This diagnostic is, to our knowledge, the first systematic demonstration of the
301   pointwise–ensemble decoupling for force-free MD, and it changes what a training or acceptance
302   criterion should measure.
303       The remedy follows from the diagnosis. A frozen backbone supplies the structure—the displace-
304   ment that determines the position marginal—while a mode-resolved generalized-Langevin readout
305   supplies the velocity that enters the next backbone call. The readout fits a per-mode AR(p) mem-
306   ory kernel to the reference velocity autocorrelation, injects noise with the fluctuation–dissipation
307   consistent variance, and rescales each mode to its canonical mean kinetic energy at every step. The
308   partition is guaranteed by construction; the memory kernel matches the multi-lag dynamics that a
309   single gain cannot. The backbone is not retrained, and the readout is fitted from a short reference

311   initial-condition independent in its partition, ACF, RDF, and VDOS, and more accurate than the
312   published checkpoint on the RDFs and VDOS in our analysis.
313       The relationship to prior work is complementary rather than competitive. TrajCast established
314   that force-free propagators can reproduce water physics 1 ; our work explains when that success
315   is fragile and adds a readout that makes the mode partition a design constraint rather than a
316   lucky outcome. Structure-preserving action maps enforce symplectic or time-reversible structure
317   and improve energy conservation and equipartition 3 ; they target mechanical structure, whereas
318   the GLR targets the canonical NVT measure and the multi-lag velocity statistics. The two could
319   be combined: an action map would supply a better backbone, and the GLR would enforce the
320   mode partition on top of it. Learned velocity updates recover stability with a black-box LSTM
321   and periodic injection 4 ; the GLR is interpretable, mode-resolved, and tied to the reference ACF,
322   and it guarantees the partition rather than only delaying divergence. GLE thermostats provide the
323   same memory-kernel and colored-noise physics 5,6 , and frequency-resolved thermostats can control
324   selected modes 7 ; the difference is the object. A GLE thermostat acts on the forces and velocities of
325   an MD integrator. The GLR is a readout on a frozen force-free propagator, fitted from the reference
326   ACF, with an explicit per-mode variance guarantee and a diagnostic that motivates it. Stochastic
327   and generative propagators offer a different route to equilibrium 8,9 , at the cost of MCMC, an energy


                                                                                                            11
328   function, or a larger generative model; the GLR is a lightweight post-hoc readout.
329       The diagnostic has implications beyond this method. Force-free propagators are usually selected
330   and reported by one-step MAE, sometimes with a short rollout score. Our results show that this
331   criterion can rank models in the wrong order. An ensemble-level, mode-resolved evaluation—kinetic
332   partition, RDF, VDOS, and diffusion—is necessary to detect the failure. The same lesson applies
333   to other learned dynamical maps: a low conditional-mean error does not imply a correct invariant
334   measure, and a stable rollout does not imply a correct one. We therefore recommend that force-free
335   MD papers report the mode partition and at least one long-time structural or dynamical observable,
336   not only one-step errors.
337       Several limitations define the boundary of the claim. First, the analytic guarantee covers the
338   mean mode kinetic partition and the velocity ACF, not the full joint position–velocity distri-
339   bution. The per-step rescale fixes the mode kinetic energy to its canonical mean and therefore
340   suppresses its equilibrium fluctuations (reference std about 0.24 eV versus about 0.002 eV in the
341   readout). The marginal velocity distribution is nevertheless close to the reference (component
342   kurtosis 2.93/5.49/5.93 versus 3.04/5.62/5.57), so the fixed-norm constraint does not distort the
343   one-point velocity statistics. The position marginal is learned by the backbone and validated empir-
344   ically; a full canonical guarantee, including the correct kinetic-energy fluctuations, would require a
345   stochastic rescale or an energy-based sampler such as MCMC. Second, the readout requires a short
346   reference NVT trajectory to fit the ACF. This is much cheaper than retraining and is available
347   for most systems of interest, but it is a data requirement, not a free lunch. The fit uses only the
348   mode velocity ACF; the RDF, VDOS, and diffusion are held out and are not used to fit any read-
349   out parameter. Third, we demonstrate one water model and one backbone family. The method
350   is backbone-agnostic in principle, but a second backbone and a second system are needed to es-
351   tablish generality. Fourth, the internal ACF at 500 fs remains too persistent for AR(10); AR(20)
352   reduces the discrepancy but does not eliminate it. Fifth, the diffusion coefficient is 26% below
353   the reference on both 2.5 ns starts (1.39–1.41 versus 1.88×10−9 ). The Green–Kubo integral shows
354   that the deficit is in the translation ACF, and the decoupled position update prevents a simple
355   blend or displacement correction from closing it; a position-consistent readout or an end-to-end
356   centre-of-mass head is future work. Sixth, the backbone uses a local 6 Å cutoff and no long-range
357   electrostatics; the GLR does not address long-range physics, and the water results should not be
358   read as a claim about large-scale dielectric or interfacial behaviour. Finally, the current production
359   runs use a single seed; seed and block error bars are needed before the numerical claims are final.
360       The outlook is straightforward. The readout can be trained jointly with the backbone, rather
361   than fitted post hoc, which may improve the position marginal while retaining the partition guar-
362   antee. The memory kernel can be made system- and temperature-adaptive, and the mode decom-
363   position can be extended to phonons or other collective coordinates. Combined with a structure-
364   preserving backbone and a long-range operator, the same diagnostic-and-readout strategy could
365   make force-free propagators reliable beyond water.

367   4.1   Reference data and force-free backbone
368   We use the flexible SPC/Fw water model 10 with 64 molecules (192 atoms) in a periodic cubic cell.
369   The NVE training data, the NVT reference, and the independent NVT trajectory are described in
370   the main text. The reference NVT trajectory is 2.5 ns at 300 K with a CSVR thermostat 2 , a 50
371   fs thermostat damping time, and a 0.5 fs saving interval; the readout is fitted on a 100 ps block
372   starting at 500 ps (20,000 frames at a 5 fs stride). The fit uses only the mode velocity ACF. The

                                                       12
373   RDF, VDOS, and diffusion are not used to fit any readout parameter and are held out as evaluation
374   observables. The independent NVT trajectory uses a different initial condition and seed: 100 ps of
375   thermalization followed by 500 ps of production at 300 K, with the first production frame at 301.8
376   K. The reference data are read-only; no reference force is used during propagation.
377        The backbone is a relational equivariant message-passing network with a 6 Å cutoff, 192 scalar
378   channels, 48 vector channels, 96 edge channels, 16 radial channels, and a 5 fs horizon. It is trained
379   on 5 fs pairs from the NVE trajectories (5,000 training, 1,250 validation, and 1,000 test graphs)
380   by minimizing a normalized endpoint position and velocity loss, with batch size 8, a learning rate
381   of 3 × 10−4 , warmup–cosine scheduling, gradient clipping at 10, and 250,000 optimizer steps. The
382   selected checkpoint is epoch 398 (2,357,616 parameters). The published TrajCast water checkpoint 1
383   has 2,258,856 parameters, uses the native e3nn backend 11 and a 5 fs horizon, and is evaluated under
384   the same 100 ps protocol with its native CSVR thermostat. In the GLR experiments the backbone
385   is frozen: no backbone parameter is updated, and the readout is fitted separately.

410   4.5   Rollout protocol and metrics
411   All rollouts use a 5 fs horizon, no external thermostat, and the same safety stop as the production
412   screens (minimum interatomic distance 0.65 Å, pre-thermostat temperature 3000 K). The 100 ps
413   screens (20,000 steps) use the val 04 NVE frame and the independent NVT frame as initial condi-
414   tions; the 2.5 ns production runs (500,000 steps) start from the same frames with no thermalization.
415   The seeds are 2026110401 (val 04), 2026110402 (independent), and 2026110401/02/03 for the seed
416   study. The published checkpoint is run with its native CSVR thermostat under the same 100 ps
417   protocol; its 2.5 ns diffusion is taken from the published value because the native float64 rollout is
418   prohibitively slow on our contended GPU.
419       The mode kinetic partition is the mean Ka over the production window. The ACF is computed
420   from the mode velocities with the same mass-weighted estimator as the fit, at lags 5–1000 fs. RDFs
421   are computed on 512 uniformly spaced frames with a 6 Å maximum radius. VDOS overlaps use the
422   same block spectral estimator as the reference: six blocks of 4.096 ps, a 100 THz Nyquist frequency,
423   and a Hann window. The diffusion coefficient is obtained from the molecular centre-of-mass mean-
424   squared displacement with time-origin averaging, eight blocks, a 50 fs sampling cadence, and a
425   linear fit over 10–100 ps per block; the global centre-of-mass drift is removed. For 2.5 ns, each
426   block is 312.5 ps long. The diffusion coefficient is D = slope/6 × 10−8 m2 s−1 when the MSD is
427   in Å2 and the lag in ps. Error bars are block means and standard deviations; the seed study uses
428   three independent noise realizations.
429       The GLR rollout costs 15.4 ns day−1 end-to-end on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell
430   GPU for 100 ps, with a peak memory of 185 MB and 2.36M parameters. The readout adds a mode
431   decomposition and a p-step history per step; it is negligible compared with the backbone forward
432   pass.

439   4.7   Statistics and reproducibility
440   The reference partition has substantial block-to-block scatter: the first 100 ps block gives 2.373/2.430/2.609
441   eV, while the 2.5 ns mean is 2.459/2.502/2.450 eV. We therefore use the 2.5 ns mean for long-time
442   partition comparisons and report the 100 ps block separately. All error bars are block means and
443   standard deviations over eight contiguous blocks unless stated otherwise. The diffusion fits use the
444   same eight-block protocol and report the block mean and standard deviation; the fit quality is sum-
445   marized by R2 . The seed study repeats the full 100 ps rollout with independent noise realizations
446   and reports the seed-to-seed spread of the partition, RDF, VDOS, and diffusion. The reference tra-
447   jectories are read-only; the rollout code writes a new HDF5 file and refuses to overwrite an existing
448   one. Each rollout records the checkpoint SHA-256, the seed, the initial frame, the integration step,
449   and the safety-stop status. No reference force is used during propagation; the reference forces are
450   used only in the independent energy verification of the NVT trajectory.

451   4.8   Computational cost
452   The GLR rollout costs 15.4 ns day−1 end-to-end on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU
453   for a 100 ps run, with a peak memory of 185 MB. The readout adds a mode decomposition and
454   a p-step history per step; the cost is negligible compared with the backbone forward pass. The
455   published native checkpoint is about an order of magnitude slower in our environment because it
456   uses float64 and the native e3nn path, which is why its 2.5 ns baseline is taken from the published
457   value rather than re-run.
